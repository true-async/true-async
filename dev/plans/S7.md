# S7 notes: the collector of coroutines that can never wake

Design note of S7.1, 2026-10-06. The collector finds the coroutines of a request that nothing can
wake any more while other coroutines still run or wait for timers and IO (a partial deadlock), and
resolves them by a policy: a warning (the default), the cancellation the global deadlock delivers,
or nothing. It is a pass over the references of the parked coroutines, PHP's trial deletion
(`zend_gc.c`) without the freeing, extended with Go's rule that a parked stack counts only once one
of its wait targets is reachable. TrueAsync has no counterpart: it detects only the global deadlock
(`scheduler.c:749-889`) and, for channels, closes soft-timer channels when the loop has nothing else
to wait for (`docs/channel-deadlock-protection.md`, layer 2); section 9 lists what S7 adds against it.

Sources: the pinned core `async-core-io-2026-10-05-4` `9531d5b0b1f` by path (`Zend/zend_gc.c`,
`Zend/zend_execute.c`, `Zend/zend_fibers.c`, `Zend/zend_types.h`, `Zend/zend_vm_def.h`); the
reference `ext/async` `1fdacf8` by file name; `dev/plans/S3.md`, `S4.md`, `S5.md`, `S6.md` below
`S3.md` and so on; the prior-art review `dev/reviews/s3-structures/prior-art.md` below `prior-art`

## 2. What can wake a parked coroutine

A coroutine parked on a wait ends its park in one of three ways: the target completes (a coroutine
finishes, a `FutureState` completes, a channel gets a value), somebody cancels it or its target
(`Coroutine::cancel()`, `Future::cancel()`, a scope's cancel in S9), or an outside source fires (a
timer, IO, a signal, another thread's trigger). The first two need code that holds the target, the
token or the coroutine: a `FutureState`, a `Future`, a `Coroutine` object, a channel, a scope. So a
parked coroutine can never wake when no outside source is armed for it and no code that can still run
reaches any of its targets or the coroutine itself.

Five ways to reach a coroutine through the registry are left out by design: `Async\get_coroutines()`,
`Async\graceful_shutdown()`, `exit()` with D16's deadline, the global deadlock, and the route of an
unhandled error through the scopes (S9, section 10). Each of them reaches every coroutine
at once and needs no reference to it; counting them would make every coroutine
reachable, as Go's leak profile would never fire if it counted `allgs`. A sixth route reaches objects
rather than coroutines: the request's end calls every destructor not yet called, whatever holds its
object (`zend_objects_store_call_destructors()`), so a destructor may complete a `FutureState` only
a found coroutine held (S7.3). The walk finds nothing once the request shuts down
(`EG_FLAGS_IN_SHUTDOWN`), the automatic runs and `get_deadlocked_coroutines()` alike. The guard
starts at the flag, before the shutdown functions, not at the destructor pass: the core's last drain
of the scheduler (whose global-deadlock cancel marks what was found during the script) runs before
the flag is set, and no drain runs between the shutdown functions and the destructors, so a
coroutine found in a shutdown function lives into the destructor pass.

The plan's edge "waiter → awaitable → completers" cannot be walked toward the completers: a
`FutureState` does not know who holds it, and a channel does not know its senders (the Critic in
S3.1, `frame-records-critic.md` section 10). Reachability answers the same question from the other
side: whoever can complete or cancel a target holds a reference to it, so a target that no running
code reaches has no completer. That is Go's answer too: the leak profile marks from the roots and
scans a blocked goroutine's stack only once something it waits on is marked (prior-art 13.6).

PHP has no list of roots: an extension may keep a value in its own globals, a resource may hold a
callable, a static property or a constant may hold an object. Trial deletion needs none. PHP's
collector subtracts the references a candidate subgraph holds to itself and keeps whatever still has a
reference left, since that reference comes from outside the subgraph (`gc_mark_grey`,
`zend_gc.c:1073`; `gc_scan`, `:1311`). The collector does the same over the subgraph the parked
coroutines reach: every holder it does not walk counts as live, so an unknown root makes the collector
miss a finding, never invent one.

## 3. The walk

### 3.1 Candidates

The walk starts from the registry (`ASYNC_G(coroutines)`) and takes as candidates the coroutines that
are SUSPENDED with at least one linked record (the waker's two and its block's, `async_wait_walk`),
every one of whose kinds has `collector_target` set (section 4). Everything else is not a candidate,
and the walk treats it as live:

- QUEUED and RUNNING coroutines: they run.
- CREATED coroutines (a core coroutine whose enqueue was refused, S3.md 7 "As built"): they wait for
  nothing.
- A wait with any record of an outside source: TIMER, TIMEOUT, the S6 IO kind, TRIGGER, and every
  kind without `collector_target` (a kind written before it learns S7 is conservative by default).
- A park with no record: `Fiber::suspend()`, a Fiber's caller in `start()`/`resume()`, the GC
  coroutine waiting for destructors (S3.md 4.8). Their edges live on the target side (the fiber's
  caller, a finish handler's waiter), which the walk does not read; Go treats unknown wait reasons the
  same way.
- A Fiber's coroutine (`ZEND_COROUTINE_F_FIBER`), parked on any wait: the `zend_fiber` holds it
  through a pointer the walk cannot count, and while its caller is parked the frame chain of its stack
  continues into the caller's (`zend_fibers.c:1006-1007`). The `zend_fiber`'s pointer is a counted
  reference in the build S7.2 runs on, so the coroutine's object stays live without the exclusion
  too, and `collector/010` passes either way; the exclusion keeps the walk off the caller's frames.

With no candidate the run ends after the registry scan.

### 3.2 Nodes and their references

A node is anything with a reference count whose holders can reach other nodes: a PHP object, a
refcounted array (immutable ones skipped), a `zend_reference`, and a refcounted event of the extension
(S4.md 2.1, `ref_count`; an event inside its object, `ASYNC_EVENT_F_ZEND_OBJ`, is that object).
Strings and resources hold no node and are never visited. A node's references are what its holder
owns:

| Node | References the walk reports |
|---|---|
| A PHP object of another class | its `get_gc` handler: the table, the count and the returned hash, as `zend_gc.c` reads them |
| An array, a reference | their values |
| A candidate coroutine | its `get_gc` (`src/coroutine.c:208-277`) and its parked stack, frame by frame from the suspended `execute_data` to the coroutine's own bottom frame (below) |
| `Future`, `FutureState` | the `FutureState` object a `Future` holds, the mapper, and the future event as its own node where the object owns a reference to it (section 10) |
| A future event | its result, its exception, the child objects of its `chain` |
| A record whose wait took a reference to its target in C (FUTURE, the token kinds, the `await_*` trigger; section 10) | its target |
| A coroutine that is not a candidate | nothing: it is live, and what it holds counts as held from outside |

The frames of a candidate's stack:

- A user function's frame goes through `zend_unfinished_execution_gc_ex` (`zend_execute.c:5028`),
  which gives its live CVs, temporaries, unfinished calls, extra arguments and `$this`.
- An internal function's frame is read by the collector itself and not passed to that function, which
  asserts that an internal frame has no extra named parameters (`:5043`) while a variadic internal
  function such as `call_user_func()` gets them (`:5566-5569`). It gives `$this` when the call owns
  it, the closure, the arguments and the extra named parameters, all owned by the call until it
  returns; so `await(spawn(...))` counts the coroutine its argument holds.
- A frame `zend_call_function()` pushed (`ZEND_CALL_TOP` with `$this` and no `ZEND_CALL_RELEASE_THIS`:
  a coroutine's body, `call_user_func()`, a destructor) gives its `$this`, which that call keeps
  until it returns (`pinned_this`, `zend_execute_API.c`) with no flag on the frame
  (`collector/050`, `052`). With `zend_execute_ex` replaced (a profiler), the VM marks its own user
  calls `ZEND_CALL_TOP` too, and a call on `$this`, `parent::` or `self::` takes no reference, so the
  rule is off for user frames and such an object counts as held from outside (`collector/054`); the
  VM never marks an internal frame so, and those keep the rule. The value is read at the run: a
  `zend_execute_ex` swapped while a frame was made (phpdbg steps that way) is a known gap.
- A generator's frame is skipped: `zend_generator_frame_gc` has no `ZEND_API`. What only that frame
  holds then counts as held from outside, so a coroutine parked inside a generator is missed when
  only the generator holds its target (`collector/017`). A frame with no
  function (a generator's `execute_fake`, `zend_generators.c:811`) is skipped before anything reads
  `func`, as `zend_unfinished_execution_gc_ex` returns on it (`zend_execute.c:5030`). A user frame
  unwinding an exception (its opline at `ZEND_HANDLE_EXCEPTION`) is skipped too: the engine finds
  its position through `EG(opline_before_exception)`, which belongs to the running context. The two
  ways user code runs during unwinding, a destructor and a generator's `finally`, give the frame its
  throwing opline back first (`zend_objects.c:158-163`, `zend_generators.c:312-318`), so a frame
  parked under them is read as any other (`collector/022`); the skip covers any other way.
- A symbol table: `EG(symbol_table)` is not reported (below); any other table gives its values once
  per walk, by its address, each `IS_INDIRECT` value read through its CV slot. `include` and `eval`
  inside a function hand the function's own table to the included frame (`zend_vm_def.h:6653-6662`),
  so two frames return the same table; reporting it twice would subtract a reference that a static
  property or a running coroutine holds.

The core declines to scan a stack parked inside an `await()` for PHP's own collector
(`zend_fibers.c:1267-1270`): that collector frees what it judges garbage, so a slot misread there is a
use after free. This collector frees nothing. A slot it leaves out hides a finding; a slot it reports
must own its reference, which holds for every slot above between the park and the wake, since the
frame changes nothing while it is parked.

A record's `event` owns no reference to its target (S3.md 4, invariant F). The reference that keeps
the target is the waiter's frame's, which is walked, or one its wait took in C and releases when the
wait ends (an internal function's local, an `await_*` block): that one no walked slot reports, so the
record's kind reports it as owned, and only such a reference. A record gives the walk a wake edge instead, target → waiter, which section 3.3
reads. The registry's own reference to each candidate is subtracted as one held inside the subgraph
(section 2).

**The global scope is a root.** Main's top frame keeps its variables in `EG(symbol_table)`, which
every coroutine reaches through `global` and `$GLOBALS`; the walk does not report that table, so what
a global variable holds stays live. Static variables, static properties, constants, resources, pending
microtasks and the stacks of running coroutines are never walked, so their references count from
outside.

**Weak references.** An object with `IS_OBJ_WEAKLY_REFERENCED` (`zend_types.h:936`) is live: a
`WeakReference::get()` or a `WeakMap` in running code can still reach it, which its reference count
does not show.

### 3.3 Counting and liveness

1. **The wait graph first.** A candidate with a record whose target is a coroutine that is not a
   candidate is live: that coroutine runs or waits for an outside source, and it can finish. Its
   waiters are then live too, to the fixed point over the wait edges alone. Only the candidates left
   go to the heap walk; a server whose coroutines wait for running workers walks nothing. A candidate
   made live here is a live node when the heap walk meets it: its registry reference is still
   subtracted, and its references are live.
2. **Count.** A walk in breadth from the candidates left, over the node table as its own queue, visits
   each node once and counts, in a side table keyed by the node's address, the references that nodes
   of the subgraph hold to it. It collects the wake edges of every candidate's records into a second
   table, target → waiters. `get_gc` answers in one buffer per thread (`zend_get_gc_buffer_create`,
   `zend_gc.c:2543-2549`), so each answer is read to its end before the next call.
3. **Seed.** A node is live when its reference count exceeds the references counted inside, when it is
   weakly referenced, when it is a coroutine that is not a candidate, or when an outside source holds
   it (3.4).
4. **Spread.** From each live node, its references are live (the walk calls the same reporters again,
   as `gc_scan_black` walks again, `zend_gc.c:872`), and a live wake-edge target makes its waiters
   live; a live candidate's object and stack are live. A worklist runs it to the fixed point.
5. **Result.** The candidates left are the coroutines that can never wake, each with the targets it
   waits for. The objects only they hold are the rest of the subgraph; they stay until their holders
   unwind (section 6).

The walk changes no reference count and writes no `GC_INFO` colour, so it never disturbs a root
buffered for PHP's own collector; its colours and counts are in its own tables. It is not free of
side effects: `zend_std_get_gc` may separate a shared properties table (`zend_array_dup`), as it does
inside PHP's collector.

A live candidate means that some running code may reach it or its target. A coroutine whose
`Coroutine` object a running coroutine or a global keeps can be cancelled, so it is never reported,
even when the holder never cancels it; a stuck coroutine handed to a supervisor stays unreported until
the supervisor drops it.

### 3.4 Soundness

A reported coroutine has no armed outside source and no reference path from code that can run to it or
its targets. A wrong report needs a reference the walk counted that its holder does not own, or a
completer that holds no counted reference. The rules that exclude both:

- A reporter gives only slots that own a reference (3.2). A slot that cannot be shown to own one is
  left out; its target then counts as held from outside, which hides a finding but reports none.
- **A source that completes a node without a counted reference seeds it.** The walk reads neither of
  the reactor's lists: a timer, an IO op, a trigger and a Timeout's timer end waits whose kind leaves
  `collector_target` NULL or reports an outside source (`async_collector_report_outside()`, the
  token kinds for a Timeout), so their waiters are never candidates. A source whose completion lands
  on an awaitable the walk counts (the reactor's `SignalHandle` and the Futures of `Async\signal()`
  waiting on it, S6.5; S10's remote Future) reports the awaitables it will complete as live
  (`async_collector_report_live_event()`) from `async_collector_find()`, which calls each such source
  before the wake edges: `async_signal_collector_seed()` (`src/os_signal.c`, `collector/032`) is the
  one source today. The guard for a source that forgets is the fuzz oracle: it completes in
  scheduler context, which excuses nothing (section 11), so a waiter it wakes after a run reported it
  aborts that source's own tests at interval 0. A seed driven by the reactor's `waits` was rejected:
  S10's remote Future completes through a trigger and a bridge to a foreign loop is on neither list,
  and an event without the op would have to make every Future waiter live, a silent miss on any
  server doing IO. A scope, a task group and anything else that may cancel coroutines it does not
  hold through PHP values (S9) holds a counted reference to each of them or reports them as live.
  S5, S6, S9 and S10 keep these rules; the Critic checks them per stage. The D16 exit deadline's
  Timer is on the reactor's `own` list, not a wait, and is not seeded.
- A count above a node's reference count means a reporter gave a reference its holder does not
  own, and a node the spread meets that the count never added means a `get_gc` answered the two
  passes differently: either way the run reports nothing (and asserts in a debug build).
- No PHP code runs between the count and the result, so no reference changes under the walk; `get_gc`
  handlers run no PHP code (`zend_gc.c` calls them inside the collection).

### 3.5 Cost

The registry scan is one pass over the registry, and the wait-graph pass is linear in the candidates'
records. The heap walk is linear in the nodes and references the remaining candidates reach, twice
(count, spread), with one side-table entry per node, as PHP's collector is linear in the subgraph of
its roots and allocates its own stack for it. A candidate whose stack reaches a large shared structure
(a `$this` that holds the application's container) walks all of it; so does PHP's collector. Section 5
bounds how often the walk runs, and section 11 measures it on 10 000 parked coroutines and on a graph
of one million nodes (B6: about 80 bytes of side table per node). The tables are on the request's
heap, as PHP's collector's stack is (`gc_stack`, `zend_gc.c`). The automatic run passes
`memory_limit` as a ceiling: before the node table and its index double, for a candidate or during
the count, and before the wake-edge table does (an edge per record: candidates awaiting the same items outnumber the nodes), a run that would take the
memory in use past it stops and finds nothing, as a run that found nothing, so the back-off grows
(`collector/057`, `collector/063`, `collector/064`). A fatal error there would end a request whose warning changes no outcome.
`get_deadlocked_coroutines()` has no ceiling: a call that needs more than `memory_limit` fails as any
other PHP call does, where an empty result would be a wrong answer. The estimate covers the node
table, its index, a worklist entry per node and the wake edges, with one heap chunk to spare; the
engine's `get_gc` buffer (an `SplObjectStorage` fills it before its elements are nodes) and the
frames' buffer (a parked frame's arguments and variables, strings among them, which are no nodes)
are not covered, as PHP's own collector does not cover the buffers it fills from `get_gc` and from
a Fiber's frames. A stopped run looks like one that found nothing.

## 4. The kind's slot

S3.md 3.5 reserved a fourth slot, `completers(record, walker)`, for this walk. Section 2 shows that
completers cannot be enumerated, so the slot reports the record's target instead and gets the name of
what it does:

```c
struct _async_wait_kind_s {
	zend_string *(*info)(const async_coroutine_event_callback_t *record);
	void (*unlink)(async_coroutine_event_callback_t *record);
	void (*abort)(async_coroutine_event_callback_t *record);
	/* Reports the record's target to the collector as a node, and whether the record owns a
	 * reference to it: whoever reaches the target can end the wait. NULL: an outside source may end
	 * the wait, and the waiter is not a candidate. */
	void (*collector_target)(const async_coroutine_event_callback_t *record, async_collector_t *collector);
};
```

The kind table stays one const table per kind in `.rodata`; nothing on a wait's path reads the slot.

| Kind | Stage | `collector_target` |
|---|---|---|
| COROUTINE | S3 | the target coroutine's object |
| TIMER, TRIGGER, the test kinds | S4 | NULL |
| FUTURE | S5 | the future event, owned: the wait holds one reference from `await()`'s C frame (S5.2) |
| token (`async_wait_kind_token`, and S5.4's Timeout token) | S5 | the token, owned for the call: a coroutine's object or the future event; a Timeout is an outside source |
| trigger (an `await_*` item) | S5 | the item, owned (`await_trigger_t`); a Timeout is never an item |
| iterator (the coroutine walking an `await_*` Traversable) | S5 | NULL: the Traversable's own code decides when it ends |
| IO | S6 | NULL |
| CHANNEL | S9 | the channel's object; a channel with no holder but its receivers is the plan's "channel without senders" |

A block's records come through its `walk` op (`src/true_async_API.h:322-326`), which S4.2 added for
this walk.

## 5. When it runs

- **At the idle point.** `scheduler_loop` (`src/scheduler.c:845`) reaches the reactor's idle wait
  when nothing is runnable and some wait has an outside source (`src/scheduler.c:862`). Before it
  blocks, the collector runs when its interval passed since its last run, on `zend_hrtime()` (S7.2:
  the idle point precedes a blocking wait, so the precise clock costs nothing there, and the tick's
  coarse clock is private to the reactor); the first idle point of a request starts the clock. The
  interval starts at `true_async.partial_deadlock_interval` milliseconds, doubles after each run
  that raised no warning up to 64 times that value, and returns to it after a run that raised one:
  under `report` the stuck coroutines stay candidates, so a run finds the old ones again, and only
  a coroutine it warns about for the first time counts. Under `cancel` a run that cancels a coroutine
  never cancelled before (`ZEND_COROUTINE_F_CANCELLED` clear) counts too, so one with `E_WARNING`
  off still resets it; a run that only cancels again a coroutine that caught the cancellation and
  parked on another dead target does not, and the interval doubles (`collector/047`). PHP's collector raises its own
  threshold when a run collects little (`gc_adjust_threshold`). The time of the last run and the
  current interval are two fields of `ASYNC_G`, read only at the idle point. A run that cancelled
  anything enqueued its coroutines, and one that warned ran an error handler that may have; the
  loop then takes another pass instead of blocking. A program that never
  idles runs it only on demand. The interval bounds how often a run happens and schedules none: the
  reactor's wait has no deadline of the collector's, so a loop blocked on one long wait runs the
  collector at its first idle point after the wait ends. A timer of its own would wake an idle server
  for a run that the back-off makes rarer each time, and would sit on the reactor's lists, which the
  walk reads.
- **On demand.** `Async\get_deadlocked_coroutines()` runs the walk in the calling coroutine and returns
  the stuck coroutines. It refuses in scheduler context and with async off, as `spawn()` and
  `get_coroutines()` do (`THROW_IF_UNAVAILABLE`, `src/true_async.c:197`): an error or output handler
  run during a tick has a SUSPENDED current coroutine with records linked. It applies no policy and
  works with the setting `off`.
- **The global deadlock stays as it is.** With nothing runnable and no outside source the scheduler
  resolves a deadlock as TrueAsync does (S3.md 6); every parked coroutine is stuck then, and the walk
  would add nothing.

Rejected:

- **With PHP's collector** (replacing `gc_collect_cycles`, `zend_gc.c:254`): it runs on the root
  buffer's fill (10 000 possible roots), which says nothing about waits, so a server idle on IO with
  one stuck coroutine might never run it; the pointer is process-wide and profilers replace it too.
- **At every park.** A park is the hot path (B1, B4); the walk is linear in the parked subgraph.
- **At every idle without a bound.** A server reaches the idle point after every IO completion that
  leaves nothing runnable.
- **A cap on the walk's nodes.** A capped run would report nothing on exactly the large graphs where a
  stuck coroutine is hardest to find by hand; the back-off bounds the cost instead, and the side table
  grows with the graph as PHP's collector's buffer does. Only `memory_limit` stops it (3.5): there the
  alternative is a fatal error that hides the finding too.
- **Persistent tables, outside `memory_limit`.** PHP's collector keeps only its root buffer
  persistent, which lives across requests; its walk counts against the limit. Persistent tables would
  hide tens of MiB per thread from the limit that protects the host, and a bailout inside the walk
  would leak them unless kept in `ASYNC_G` for reuse.

## 6. Policy

`true_async.partial_deadlock` (`PHP_INI_ALL`) decides what an automatic run does with each stuck
coroutine:

- `report` (default): one `E_WARNING` per stuck coroutine, "Partial deadlock: coroutine #%u spawned
  at %s:%u can never wake (%s)", the last field its first awaiting-info line (S4.md 2.4); without
  " spawned at %s:%u" for a coroutine with no spawn file (main, or a spawn while no PHP code ran).
  Once per coroutine: bit 22 of the flags word, `ASYNC_COROUTINE_F_DEADLOCK_REPORTED`, is set when
  the warning is raised and never cleared, since a stuck coroutine stays stuck. With `E_WARNING` out
  of `error_reporting` nothing is raised and the bit stays clear, so a later run warns once it is
  back; only a run that warned resets the back-off. The bit gates the warning only. The
  coroutines stay parked, so when the rest of the request finishes the global deadlock resolves them
  as in TrueAsync, with its `DeadlockError`.
- `cancel`: the warning (once), then every stuck coroutine is cancelled with
  `AsyncCancellation("Deadlock detected")`, protection cleared, as the global deadlock cancels each
  waiter (`scheduler_resolve_deadlock`, TrueAsync's `scheduler.c:860-879`); no exit exception, since
  the rest of the request goes on. Each run that finds a coroutine cancels it, so one that catches the
  cancellation and parks on another dead target is cancelled again. Main is warned about and left
  parked: an `AsyncCancellation` main does not catch ends the script silently with exit status 0,
  since the unobserved-exception rule skips cancellations, while parked main meets the global
  deadlock and its `DeadlockError` once the rest of the request ends (`collector/046`). A graceful
  shutdown in progress changes nothing: what it cancelled is queued, not parked, and the run skips it.
- `off`: no automatic run.

The warning is raised in scheduler context, where the global deadlock report is written too, and
only when `error_reporting` read from INI includes `E_WARNING`, as the deadlock report reads it
(`ini_error_reporting()`, `src/scheduler.c:750-751`): the scheduler's stack has its own copy of the
setting. An exception an error handler throws (a framework turning warnings into `ErrorException`) is
released: no PHP code called what warned, so nothing could catch it, and joining it to the exit
exception would end a long-running request on its first finding. An error handler that waits gets the
scheduler-context `Error`, released the same way. An `exit()` there ends the request as one in a
coroutine does (D16, `collector/024`), and a fatal error ends it by the bailout (`collector/025`).

**Why `report` is the default.** TrueAsync never resolves a partial deadlock: its stuck coroutines
wait until the global deadlock, and a long-running server keeps serving (P2.2). A warning changes no
outcome and is what the leak profile Go ships gives (prior-art 13.6). `cancel` is opt-in.

**Why a cancellation, not `DeadlockError`.** The plan named `DeadlockError` thrown into the waiters.
A stuck coroutine is by definition one nobody holds, so a `DeadlockError` it does not catch meets the
unobserved-exception rule of its finalize (`src/coroutine.c:454-461`), becomes the exit exception and
cancels every other coroutine: a server would stop on its first finding. The global deadlock already
splits the two the same way, `DeadlockError` for the request and `AsyncCancellation` for each waiter;
`cancel` takes the second half.

Every stuck coroutine is cancelled in the same run, as the global deadlock cancels every waiter. One of
them may hold, in a `finally` block, what would have woken another (a `FutureState` completed while it
unwinds); the other is cancelled even so, as in the global deadlock. Cancelling only the coroutines
whose targets no other stuck coroutine holds was rejected: it needs a reachability pass per stuck
coroutine, and two coroutines each holding the other's target would be skipped forever.

What the stuck coroutines alone hold (a `Future` whose `FutureState` is gone, a channel with no sender)
is released by reference counting as `cancel` unwinds their stacks; under `report` and `off` it stays
until the global deadlock or the request's end, as today.

## 7. PHP's collector

PHP's collector never frees a parked coroutine: the registry holds a reference it cannot explain, and
the coroutine's `get_gc` leaves the parked stack out (`src/coroutine.c:272-273`), so two coroutines
awaiting each other, and everything their frames hold, stay until the request ends. The core walks a
Fiber's stack only when it is parked in `Fiber::suspend()` (`zend_fibers.c:1267-1305`), and not inside
an `await()`. The collector finds exactly those cycles and frees nothing itself: `cancel` unwinds the
frames, and reference counting and PHP's collector then free what they held. The two never run at
once: the collector runs at the idle point or in a PHP function, PHP's collector in its own coroutine,
and neither runs PHP code while it walks.

## 8. PHP API

```php

/** The coroutines nothing can wake any more (dev/plans/S7.md); the walk runs now. */
```

It returns a list of `Coroutine` objects in registry order; holding the list makes them reachable, so
the next run does not report them again until the caller drops it, and the caller may cancel them. INI,
both `PHP_INI_ALL`:

| Setting | Default | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `true_async.partial_deadlock` | `report` | section 6; `off`, `0` and the empty string (a bare `off` in `php.ini`, which the INI parser reads as a boolean) are `off`; `cancel` |
| `true_async.partial_deadlock_interval` | `5000` | milliseconds before the first automatic run and between runs, before the back-off; at least `1000`, or `0`, which runs at every idle point without back-off (tests, fuzz) |

**A Scope's reach** (S7.7, agreed with Edmond and the S9 thread; S9-scope.md 6). A scope belongs to
no one: its coroutines' list or a variable keeps it, and a coroutine's +1 belongs to the scheduler, so
the walk never follows a scope's members as references (Edmond, 2026-10-07). One rule is counted: code
that can still run and holds a `Scope` object can cancel that scope and its child scopes, so their
coroutines are reachable, as a held `Coroutine` object makes its coroutine reachable through
`cancel()`. The membership vector keeps bare pointers, as TrueAsync's (`scope.c:47`), so the rule
enters the walk as **reach nodes**, nodes no count makes live (`COLLECTOR_NODE_REACH`: the count and
the spread neither read a refcount of them nor walk their references):

1. Before the wake edges, S9's `async_scope_collector_reach()` climbs from each candidate's scope to
   the root and gives each scope it meets one reach node, keyed by the scope's member vector
   (`async_collector_reach_node()`), with an edge of the wake-edge table from the scope's node to the
   candidate or to the child scope's node it came from (`async_collector_report_reach()`). A scope met
   before stops the climb, since its parents are linked already; a run so costs the candidates plus
   the scopes they are in.
2. A scope that still has its object, a stand-in included, reports the object as the node's holder
   (`async_collector_report_holder()`). After the count each holder is looked up in the index
   (`zend_hash_index_find`): one the walk never met is held from outside, or by garbage whose
   destructor will cancel the scope, so the scope's node is `KNOWN_LIVE`; one it met gets an edge to
   the scope's node, and the spread decides. The `await_*` iterator coroutine is reported the same
   way as the holder of its own scope, which it cancels when the walk throws (S9.9, S9-scope.md 6).
3. A `Scope` object reports nothing (`collector_object_references()` returns for `async_ce_scope`):
   its exception handlers belong to the internal scope and only the route below calls them.

A scope with no live object, no live parent's object and no live `await_*` walk reaches nothing,
and a coroutine that is no candidate is live already, so neither needs a node. Every node and edge
is under the memory ceiling; a failed run passes `COLLECTOR_NONE` through. The SCOPE kind's
`collector_target` (S9.9) reports a reach node per awaited scope, live once a coroutine of its
subtree is (`async_collector_report_reach_target()`, `async_collector_report_reach_source()`;
S9-scope.md 6).

**An unhandled error's route is left out by design**, at every level, as the registry's walks are
(section 2): the route cancels each level's subtree with the origin's safe flag, read when the error
happens, and any live coroutine of a tree can make an unsafe origin later, so counting it would make
a tree all or nothing: a server's `new Scope()` with a live accept loop would never find a stuck
handler. DECISIONS 2026-10-07, reversible. A `SpawnStrategy` whose `provideScope()` returns null
is left out for the same reason: its hooks get the object of the current scope, a stand-in when it
has none, so any live member can cancel its own scope (the Critic). For the oracle, in test-hook
builds each route level, before its handler and its cancel, marks the found coroutines of its
subtree handed out (`scope_hand_out_found()`), as `registry_cancel()` marks what it cancels; the
level that takes the error ends the marking, so the levels above keep the oracle. `spawn_with()`
marks the current scope's subtree before the hooks only when `provideScope()` returned null: a Scope
the provider returns is held, so nothing in its subtree is found. Marking once is enough: while the
hooks run, `spawn_with_strategy()`'s own reference to the object is one the count never sees, and a
handler cannot park. Tests `collector/065`-`072`.

**Which references to the future event exist** is fixed against S5's code in S7.3, before its
reporters are written, since the notes read two ways: S5.md 2 says a waiter and a drain hold references
to the event, while S3.md 4 invariant F says the waiter's frame keeps the target alive; S3.md 4 says
one `Z_ADDREF` per `await_*` item, while S5.md 5 says the input array keeps the items. S5.2's code
settles the first: `await()` on a Future holds one reference to the event in its C frame for the whole
wait (`async_future_await()`), which the FUTURE record reports as owned. The rule for
each: a reference that S5 holds from C is reported exactly once, by the holder the walk reaches (the
FUTURE record as owned, a `FutureState` object for its event); a reference nobody holds is not
reported. `new Future($state)` holds the `FutureState` object and no reference to the event (S5.md 2);
a child of `map()` and the Future of `completed()` hold the event themselves.

**The holders of a future event** (S7.3, from S5's code; `base.ref_count` counts all of them):

| Holder | Reference | Reported by |
|---|---|---|
| `FutureState` | one, its own event | `async_future_collector_references()` (`src/future.c`), as an event node |
| `Future` on a `FutureState` | none: it holds the state object | the same function, the state object |
| `Future` of `map()`, `catch()`, `finally()`, `completed()`, `failed()` | one, its own event | the same function, as an event node |
| A source event's `chain` | one per child Future object | the event's reporter, the child objects |
| `await()` on a Future | one for the wait, in the C frame | FUTURE's `collector_target`, owned |
| An `await_*` trigger (`await_trigger_t`) | one per pending item | the trigger kind's `collector_target`, owned |
| A token, for the call that waits with it | one (`async_awaitable_addref`), in the C frame | the token kind's `collector_target`, owned |
| A drain (mappers being called) | one per parent and child | nobody: outside, so live |

`get_gc` of the two classes folds the event into its only holder (`future_event_gc`, `ref_count == 1`)
and reports nothing while a waiter holds it too, which PHP's collector needs and this walk does not:
the walk calls `async_future_collector_references()` instead for these final classes. A Timeout
(S5.4) as a token is reported as an outside source
(`async_collector_report_outside()`), since its timer may fire; an `await_*` over a Traversable has a
record on its iterator coroutine, whose kind keeps `collector_target` NULL, so its waiter is never a
candidate. A `signal()` Future's event is borrowed by its wait in the watch (S6.5), so its Future object is
its only counted holder: the watch seeds it live instead (3.4).

**The fuzz oracle** (S7.2): in every build with the test hooks a reported coroutine keeps its mark
until a cancellation is applied to it; one woken by a record's wake (its target completed) while
marked fails the run as a false report, and so does a cancel of a marked coroutine from anywhere but
the walks over the registry (`registry_cancel()`, which marks what it cancels as handed out), the
`cancel` policy (which marks it the same way) and the bailout, unless it or a coroutine its wait targets was handed out by
`get_coroutines()` or `get_deadlocked_coroutines()` (a test-build bit set there), since cancels
through the registry are left out by design (section 2). A coroutine woken by a target that was
handed out, or that a bailout ended, is excused too, and passes the excuse on to its own waiters.
Since S7.3 the wakes of the event kinds (FUTURE, the tokens, the `await_*` triggers) check the same:
their completer is the running coroutine, excused only in the bailout; one handed out was live at
the run, and whatever cancels the found coroutines hands the waiter out itself. Scheduler context,
where outside sources complete, gives no excuse. `TrueAsync\Test\mark_found()` marks a coroutine
without handing it out, so `collector/040` shows each rule in a child process. A
false report aborts the process, which fails the seed. Missed findings are not its concern. Since
`get_deadlocked_coroutines()` hands out what it returns, the oracle checks only the automatic runs
of the fuzz, not the tests that call it. The S3-S7 lists run under it with `report` and the interval
at 0, on 10 seeds in S7.2 and 100 in S7.5. A warning in a ported test's output is a true finding
that changes that test; it goes to the plan with the test named.

`get_deadlocked_coroutines()` over 10 000 coroutines parked in pairs that await each other, over the
